# Reflection

I chose timing side channels because they connect two ideas from the course: algorithmic complexity and information leakage. It is easy to think of complexity only as a question of how fast an algorithm runs as its input grows. Building this visualization helped me see that the amount of work can also depend on secret data, and an observer may learn something from that difference even when the secret is never directly printed.

The interactive comparison makes this idea concrete. I can submit guesses with different numbers of leading characters matching the demo token and observe how the early-exit model's timing changes. Comparing it with a fixed-work model shows why checking every position can reduce one source of timing leakage. The measurement history and optional synthetic noise also show that timing is an observation rather than a perfect signal: noise can obscure a pattern, and real systems introduce much more complexity than this simplified example.

My workflow was AI-assisted and iterative. I used AI to brainstorm a safe topic, plan the interface, draft a single-file HTML/CSS/JavaScript app, and prepare the README. I then focused on whether the controls supported the learning goal where a learner chooses a guess, explicitly measures it, compares the two implementations, and tests their understanding with a quiz. Keeping everything in one HTML file makes it easy to run and inspect without installing dependencies.

The main lesson I took away is that asymptotic complexity and security are related but not interchangeable. Big-Theta notation describes how work scales, while a side-channel analysis asks whether observable behavior reveals sensitive information. Fixed-work comparison is a useful design principle, but it is not a universal guarantee of security; real implementations must also consider compilers, memory behavior, and hardware. AI helped me prototype quickly, but I still needed to consider whether the model was clear, accurate, and appropriately limited for an educational setting.
